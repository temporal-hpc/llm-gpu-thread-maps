The list you posted is just a very long concatenation of consecutive integers that all appear in the same range (1 … 1000).  
Because the sum of an arithmetic progression

\[
1+2+3+\dots+n \;=\;\frac{n\,(n+1)}{2}
\]

does **not** depend on the order in which the terms are written, you can avoid summing each element one by one and simply apply the formula.

* For \(n = 1000\)

\[
\text{sum} = \frac{1000\,(1000+1)}{2}= \frac{1000\times 1001}{2}=500\,500 .
\]

* If every integer from 1 to 1000 occurs several times – say \(k\) times each – the total sum is simply

\[
k \times \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]

So for instance, if the whole list contains each number 1‑to‑1000 exactly 3 times, the sum would be

\[
3 \times 500\,500 = 1\,501\,500 .
\]

When the numbers themselves (or the total count) become large enough that the sum no longer fits into a 32‑ or 64‑bit integer, just use a big‑integer type (e.g. `java.math.BigInteger`, `C# System.Numerics.BigInteger`, etc.) – the arithmetic‑series formula works with arbitrary‑size integers just as well.

In short, if the sequence is a simple arithmetic progression or a known repetition of one, use the closed‑form formula above. If the sequence is arbitrary, you still have to add the elements (but you can do it in a program, not by hand).
