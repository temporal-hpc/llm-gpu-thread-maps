**Answer – 359 251 200**  

---

### 1.  What is being counted?

We have the multiset of letters  

\[
\{A,B,\,C,C,\,D,E,F,G,H,I,J,K,L,M,\,N,N,N,\,R,R\}
\]

(12 different singletons and the three repeated letters C, R and N).  
We are asked to form a **10‑letter string** that

* contains **both** Cs,
* contains **both** Rs,
* contains **at least two** Ns (so we may use either 2 or all 3 Ns).

All other letters may appear or not, but a 10‑letter word has to be chosen.

---

### 2.  Choose the letters that will appear

A 10‑letter word must use

* both Cs (2 letters),
* both Rs (2 letters),
* exactly two Ns (otherwise we would have 11 letters),  
  so the third N is left out.

That leaves \(10-2-2-2=4\) positions to be filled with distinct letters from the 12 singletons  
\( \{A,B,D,E,F,G,H,I,J,K,L,M\}\).

The number of ways to pick the 4 distinct singletons is

\[
\binom{12}{4}=495 .
\]

---

### 3.  Arrange the chosen letters

We now have the multiset  

\[
\{C,C,\,R,R,\,N,N,\,\text{4 distinct letters}\}
\]

for a total of 10 letters.  
The number of distinct permutations of a multiset is

\[
\frac{10!}{2!\,2!\,2!}= \frac{3\,628\,800}{8}=453\,600 .
\]

---

### 4.  Multiply the choices

For each of the 495 choices of the 4 distinct singletons we have 453 600 different
arrangements, so

\[
495 \times 453\,600 = 359\,251\,200 .
\]

---

### 5.  Why the answer is not 1 (or 2)

The reasoning above shows that the constraints are **not** restrictive – any
10‑letter word that uses both Cs and both Rs automatically has at least two Ns.
Hence there are **hundreds of millions** of possible strings, not a single one.

(If instead the word had to use all 19 letters, the number of distinct
permutations would be  
\( \displaystyle \frac{19!}{2!\,3!\,2!}=5\,068\,541\,275\,368\,000\),  
which also satisfies the same conditions.)

---

**Therefore, the number of different 10‑letter strings that contain both Cs, both Rs
and at least two Ns is**

\[
\boxed{359\,251\,200}
\]
